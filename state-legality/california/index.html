What California law requires of the equipment

California is one of ten states that write certification into the statute. This section is about what satisfies it, not about what we can earn from.

Cal. Pub. Util. Code §8530(c)(5) — “Is certified as a plug-in photovoltaic system by Underwriters Laboratories, or an equivalent nationally recognized testing laboratory.”
§8530(c)(6) — “Includes a feature, certified by Underwriters Laboratories or an equivalent nationally recognized testing laboratory, that isolates the portable solar generation device from the building’s electrical system to prevent the portable solar generation device from backfeeding electricity to the electrical grid during a power outage.” from 1 January 2027

Two different jobs. Make sure you want the right one.

A grid-tie kit feeds power into your home while the sun is up, which lowers what you draw from the utility. It shuts down in a blackout by design, so that line workers are not exposed to your power.

A power station does the opposite. It will run your fridge in an outage and it lowers nothing on an ordinary day.

Most people who arrive on this page want the first one. If you want backup as well, that is a second purchase, not a bigger version of the first.

California's high electricity prices make even a small kit attractive, and its solar framework is mature. SB 868 is now law, but it does not apply until 1 January 2027: until then grid-tied systems fall under standard interconnection rules, and most utilities expect generation to be registered.

SB 868 — what the new law does

SB 868 (Sen. Wiener) was signed on 30 September 2026 and filed with the Secretary of State the same day as Chapter 985, Statutes of 2026. It adds Sections 8530 to 8532 to the Public Utilities Code. From 1 January 2027 a portable solar generation device is exempt from all interconnection requirements imposed by state law, the Public Utilities Commission, electrical corporation rules or local publicly owned electric utility rules (§8531(a)). To qualify, a device must meet all six conditions in §8530(c):

  • a maximum aggregated AC output of no more than 1,200 watts per dwelling;
  • designed to be connected to and disconnected from a building’s electrical system through a receptacle;
  • intended to offset the customer’s onsite electricity consumption;
  • meets the most recent National Electrical Code and the California Electrical Code;
  • certified as a plug-in photovoltaic system by UL or an equivalent nationally recognized testing laboratory;
  • includes a certified feature that stops it backfeeding the grid during a power outage.

Your utility may not require approval, a fee or charge, or extra controls or equipment (§8531(b)). It may ask you to report the address, make, model and size through a simple online registration form, and that notification “shall not require approval” (§8531(c)). A device that misses any of the six conditions does not qualify (§8531(d)).

When it starts, and when it ends

The act states no operative date. Under the California Constitution, Art. IV §8(c)(1), a statute goes into effect on January 1 next following a 90-day period from enactment, so SB 868 takes effect on 1 January 2027.

The exemption carries a sunset, quoted in full: “This section shall remain in effect only until January 1, 2030, and as of that date is repealed.” (§8531(e)). From the same date, §8532 bars the sale of a receptacle-connected plug-in device that does not meet the code, certification and anti-backfeed conditions. California is the only state in our tracker whose plug-in exemption is time-limited.

Before you buy in California

Until 1 January 2027, call your electric utility and ask whether they allow small plug-in grid-tied solar and what notification or interconnection form they require; stay within their wattage, use a UL-listed microinverter, and keep written confirmation. From 1 January 2027, check the device against the six conditions above — the certification is the one most kits do not state.
