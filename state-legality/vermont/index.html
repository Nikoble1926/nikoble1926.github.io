Is Plug-In Solar Legal in Vermont?

Legal — signed (S.202, effective Jul 1, 2026)   Avg rate ~21¢/kWh   Updated: July 2, 2026

Last verified: August 3, 2026 · source data

Quick answer: Yes. Vermont’s S.202 was signed by Gov. Phil Scott on June 16, 2026, making Vermont the 7th U.S. state to legalize plug-in (balcony) solar, and its plug-in provisions took effect July 1, 2026. Customers can use plug-in solar devices up to a combined 1,200W per electric meter with equipment certified by a nationally recognized testing laboratory, without an interconnection agreement or extra utility fees. Average residential electricity rate is around 21¢/kWh.
Vermont law names the standard. Act 149 (S.202) writes the certification into the definition: 30 V.S.A. § 201(9)(C) says a plug-in photovoltaic device is one that “complies with UL 3700 for plug-in photovoltaic systems by UL Solutions or an equivalent certification by an equivalent Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory for use in the United States” and operates to IEEE 1547-2018. A device that does not meet it is not a plug-in photovoltaic device under the Act, so it never reaches the exemption and stays under section 248, Vermont’s certificate of public good process — and an interconnection agreement. You also need a smart meter. Act 149 Sec. 2 (30 V.S.A. § 256(a)) states that plug-in photovoltaic devices “shall only be connected to systems using smart meters”, and that the customer must keep the device “temporarily but securely attached to the ground or a structure”. Without a smart meter the exemption does not apply, however well certified the kit is. Green Mountain Power can tell you which meter you have.

Vermont’s S.202 (“an act relating to plug-in photovoltaic devices”) was signed into law by Gov. Phil Scott on June 16, 2026 — making Vermont the 7th U.S. state to legalize plug-in / balcony solar — and its plug-in provisions took effect on July 1, 2026. The law allows plug-in solar devices up to a combined 1,200 watts per electric meter, using equipment certified by a nationally recognized testing laboratory (UL or equivalent), without the section 248, Vermont’s certificate of public good process, an interconnection agreement, or extra utility fees. In practice: choose a certified (UL 3700 pathway) kit, stay within the 1,200W cap, and note that the Act requires a smart meter, so check yours with your utility — in Vermont residential power mostly comes from Green Mountain Power. With roughly average electricity prices, a plug-in kit delivers steady savings and a typical payback in the mid-single-digit years. Before you buy in Vermont

Since July 1, 2026 you don’t need utility approval for a compliant device, but do two quick checks: (1) confirm with your utility (e.g. Green Mountain Power) whether your meter meets the law’s smart-meter condition, and (2) make sure the kit is certified by a nationally recognized testing laboratory (UL or equivalent) and your total stays within the 1,200W-per-meter cap.
